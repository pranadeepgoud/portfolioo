Notes from the queue

Short write-ups on patterns I see often, written for anyone working in fraud or AML.

AML · Mules3 min read

Five signs an account is being used as a money mule

Mule accounts rarely look criminal on their own. The pattern shows up when you compare what the account does with who the customer says they are.

  1. Money in, money out. Funds leave within minutes or hours of arriving, and the balance keeps going back to near zero.
  2. Many unrelated senders. Inbound payments come from people with no visible link to the customer or to each other.
  3. Activity that doesn't fit the profile. A student or a low-income customer suddenly moves amounts far above their declared income.
  4. Shared access. The same device, IP address or login pattern appears on several unconnected accounts.
  5. Fast cash-out. Onward transfers go to crypto platforms, cash withdrawals or new payees in other countries.

One sign is a question. Three together is usually a case.

Fraud · APP3 min read

Why APP scams slip past transaction monitoring

In an authorised push payment scam, the victim logs in, passes every security check and sends the money themselves. To a rules engine, it looks like a normal customer making a normal payment.

The signals are in behaviour, not authentication:

  • A first payment to a new payee that is much larger than anything the customer usually sends.
  • Several attempts in a row, or a payment split into smaller amounts after a block.
  • Urgency, such as a customer insisting the payment must go today, or mentioning a "bank" or "police" caller.
  • A receiving account that already shows mule patterns.

The best defence is speed: a well-timed warning before the payment, and a fast recall request to the receiving bank after it.

EDD · KYC3 min read

Source of funds and source of wealth are not the same check

Source of funds asks where the money behind a specific transaction or balance came from. Source of wealth asks how the customer built their total net worth over time.

Mixing them up causes two common mistakes:

  • Accepting a payslip as source of funds when the deposit in question is ten times a monthly salary.
  • Asking for proof of wealth when one sale contract would explain the single payment under review.

Getting the question right also means asking customers for less. Check what is already on file and in open sources first, then request only the document that closes the gap.

A precise request gets a faster answer and a decision that holds up under review.
